Issue 5 2026-10-12 Week of Oct 6 – 12, 2026 6 top stories

This week in malware

Welcome to Virus Watch Weekly #5. Research for this issue was pulled from public reporting on Monday, Oct 12, 2026 (America/Denver). This week's theme: malware that hides where you'd least expect it — inside brand-new budget Android phones, inside thousands of fake GitHub projects, and inside stealers that now go after AI assistant logins. Plus a takedown-resistant Windows loader, North Korea-linked ransomware hidden in a résumé, and a brand-new extortion gang.

Top Stories

Midnight Mimosa: malware pre-installed in the firmware of budget Android phones

High
Midnight Mimosa (firmware proxyware)
In plain English

Some cheap Android phones and tablets are infected before you ever turn them on — the bad code is baked into the phone's built-in software at the factory or supply chain. It quietly uses your phone and internet connection to click fake ads and to rent your connection out to strangers, and it can install or remove apps without asking. You can't fix it by deleting an app. Buy phones from well-known brands and reputable sellers, keep them updated, and be wary of no-name bargain devices.

Bitdefender (report Oct 8, updated Oct 9) found Midnight Mimosa embedded as a system package in firmware of low-cost Android devices from several brands on MediaTek platforms. It monetizes via ad fraud and residential proxy services and can silently install/remove apps and load new code. Thousands of devices in 150+ countries over ~2 years (Mexico, France, Italy, US, Germany, Brazil, Spain led). Bitdefender corrected one indicator (85.17.70.38:6000) as a sinkhole, not live C2.

Platforms: Android (budget MediaTek devices)
How it spreads: Supply chain / pre-installed firmware
Source: Bitdefender via Cyber Security News

FakeGit returns: 17,610 fake GitHub projects push SmartLoader and the StealC password stealer

High
FakeGit / SmartLoader / StealC
In plain English

Criminals run thousands of copycat project pages on GitHub (a popular site where programmers share code), many posing as AI tools. The big “Download” button gives you a ZIP file that installs a password stealer. It grabs saved passwords and even your logged-in sessions, which can let attackers into accounts without needing your password or two-factor code. Only download software from the official project or vendor page, and be suspicious of a GitHub page whose main feature is a download button.

Apiiro (reported by BleepingComputer Oct 8) says FakeGit reactivated Oct 4 across 17,610 repos, re-pointing 13,000+ in 34 hours (peak 2,999/hour). 97% of sampled commits only changed the README; 88% pointed Download at a ZIP installing SmartLoader, which reads its C2 from a Polygon smart contract and deploys StealC. Many repos impersonate AI skills/MCP servers; ~700 accounts appear to belong to legitimate developers. 71% of the fleet was missing from URLhaus before the report.

Platforms: Windows
How it spreads: Fake GitHub repositories → ZIP download → SmartLoader → StealC
Source: BleepingComputer / Apiiro

Warden Stealer: fast-growing password thief that also steals AI assistant logins

High
Warden Stealer (aka CallbackBeaver)
In plain English

Warden is a password-stealing program sold to criminals as a service, and it's now one of the most common ones seen. It spreads through pirated software, game cheats, fake ads and “paste this command” tricks. Besides browser passwords and crypto wallets, it steals the login keys and chat histories of AI assistants and coding tools, and it can swap a crypto address you copy for the attacker's. Avoid cracked software and cheats, never paste commands a website gives you, and sign out/reset AI tool keys if a PC gets infected.

Gen Threat Labs (SecurityOnline, Oct 12) links CallbackBeaver and Warden Stealer, a Rust MaaS stealer advertised since Aug 2026 (early builds May). Targets Windows 8–11; ranks alongside Vidar, Amatera and Remus in Gen telemetry. Includes loader and crypto clipper, Chrome ABE bypass, and (since v1.9) theft of config/tokens/MCP credentials/prompt histories from AI assistants and agentic coding tools.

Platforms: Windows
How it spreads: Cracked software, game cheats, malvertising, ClickFix
Source: SecurityOnline / Gen Threat Labs

AvisLoader: a Windows loader that talks through an encrypted chat network to survive takedowns

Med
AvisLoader
In plain English

AvisLoader is a “foot in the door” program: once it's on a PC it lets criminals install whatever they want next. It gets in through a fake web page that tells you to paste a command to fix something. Instead of contacting one server that police could seize, it takes orders over Tox, an encrypted peer-to-peer chat network, so there's nothing simple to shut down. Again: no legitimate site needs you to paste commands into Windows.

Varonis Threat Labs (BleepingComputer, Oct 12) found AvisLoader on an exposed staging server with a ClickFix lure and an 'AvisLoader Command Center' panel. 64-bit loader statically links c-toxcore and uses Tox P2P for C2 and payload delivery; panel supports shell tasks, victim targeting by location/hardware, and file staging. Toolkit includes shortcut persistence (VLCAssistant), a UAC-bypass helper and a process-hiding DLL. Advertised on a cybercrime forum.

Platforms: Windows
How it spreads: ClickFix lure → loader → follow-on payloads
Source: Varonis / BleepingComputer

Funhouse ransomware linked to North Korea's Kimsuky arrives in a fake résumé

High
Funhouse (Endpoint ransomware family)
In plain English

A North Korea-linked hacking group is sending fake job résumés as documents. Clicking a link inside sets off a chain of hidden steps that first installs a remote-control tool and then ransomware that locks your files (they end in “.Funhouse”) and demands payment. Treat unexpected résumés and attachments with care, especially ones asking you to click a link inside the document, and keep offline backups.

AhnLab ASEC (Oct 12) analyzed Funhouse, an evolution of the Endpoint ransomware family, distributed via a malicious Hangul (HWP) résumé with an in-document hyperlink launching Batch/VBS/PowerShell/Python and bitmap-embedded stages; a Quasar RAT variant loads the ransomware. HC-128 encryption, .Funhouse extension, note 'How To Restore Your Files.Txt', contact funhouse8764@tutamail.com. Attributed to Kimsuky with medium confidence. Primarily Korean targets.

Platforms: Windows
How it spreads: Malicious HWP résumé → multi-stage scripts → Quasar RAT → ransomware
Source: AhnLab ASEC (via lazarus.day)

Umbra: brand-new extortion gang posts nine victims in three days

Med
Umbra (extortion group)
In plain English

A new criminal group called Umbra appeared on Oct 6 and quickly claimed nine victims — universities, tech, energy, retail and food companies across the Middle East, South Asia and Latin America. Nobody yet knows how it breaks in. It's a reminder that new gangs pop up constantly; the basics (updates, multi-factor sign-in, backups) protect against them all.

Orbitaley (Oct 8), using RansomLook data, reports Umbra's first leak-site posts on Oct 6, reaching nine claims (Egypt, Saudi Arabia, India, Brazil, others). No vendor analysis of access vectors or tooling yet; an unrelated Rust encryptor named Umbra (Aug 2026) is not confirmed to be linked. Claims are unverified.

Platforms: Unknown
How it spreads: Unknown
Source: Orbitaley / RansomLook

Quick Hits

  • BlossCraft Launcher (reported Oct 2, tracked Oct 10): a fake Minecraft-style game launcher that steals browser passwords, Discord and gaming sessions, and Wi-Fi passwords. Kids' and gamers' PCs are the target — only download games from official stores.
  • Akira ransomware (Huntress, Oct 8): responders rebuilt an Akira attack that used a disguised “svchost.exe” tunnel, Rclone to copy stolen data to the cloud, and deleted backups before encrypting file shares.
  • ClingSTUN follow-up (Oct 6): more analysis says it exploits 24 known router/camera bugs dating back to 2014 — another nudge to update or replace old home and office gadgets.
